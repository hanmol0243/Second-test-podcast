Weather.

Tonight is a clear seventy-two degrees, Thursday reaches seventy-nine, Friday seventy-two, and Saturday dawn dips to fifty-five. For Boca Raton that is a cold snap, so you will dig out a jacket for fifty-five degrees and describe it as brutal to everyone you meet.

Here are the biggest news stories and how they connect. An energy shock, a strong economy and a tariff wave are all pushing the same way. The International Energy Agency calls the Hormuz crisis the largest energy supply shock in history, and US consumer prices are up three point eight percent on the year, the highest since May. Third-quarter growth was revised up to four point three percent, so the Federal Reserve cannot treat this as a pure supply shock, and a December hike is widely expected. Today the Nasdaq was down almost four percent in early trading on doubts about the AI boom. My reading: a higher discount rate is repricing long-dated AI profits, just as new tariffs add another cost to pass on.

In Yemen, the Houthis claimed strikes on the Saudi Red Sea ports of Jeddah and Jazan, and explosions rocked Riyadh, in what they call retaliation for Saudi-led air strikes.

Economy and markets.

Federal Reserve. Third-quarter growth was revised up to four point three percent annualised, from three point four. October consumer prices rose three point eight percent on the year, on soaring energy costs. A December rate rise is widely expected, so markets already treat it as the base case.

The mechanism is the policy rate, which the Fed lifts to raise borrowing costs and cool demand. The intended effect is to stop an energy shock hardening into inflation expectations. A Taylor rule, which sets rates from the inflation gap and the output gap, points upward when both are positive. The risk is that energy is a supply shock, which central banks usually look through, and a hike on top of lost real income could tip growth over. Economists disagree: hawks say growth above four percent shows demand, not just oil, is running hot; doves say a hike taxes households already paying more for fuel.

The read-through is tighter financial conditions everywhere. My inference: the dollar is supported, and long-duration assets pay first. That is the Nasdaq story. Higher rates cut the present value of profits that arrive late, which is exactly how the AI trade is valued, so the sell-off is part repricing and part doubt, and today's data cannot separate them.

Tariffs. The US trade representative proposed new tariffs on fourteen partners under Section three hundred and one of the Trade Act, after an overcapacity investigation. Rates are fifteen percent for the EU, Taiwan and South Korea, eighteen for most others, twenty-five for India and thirty-five for Vietnam. China faces twenty percent on top of existing duties, about forty in total. They could start early next year, and the US-China trade truce expires in January.

Business.

Poundland has collapsed into administration, a year after an American owner bought it for one pound. More than ten thousand jobs are at risk and the chain is expected to be broken up.

Moody's said Huang's reinstatement eases uncertainty at Nvidia and left its ratings unchanged.

Hellman and Friedman raised sixteen billion dollars for its ninth fund, according to an SEC filing.

Science and technology.

Europe's Hera spacecraft sent the first close-up images of the crater NASA's DART probe punched into an asteroid, and scientists say the debris field is a bigger mess than expected. China launched Shenzhou-twenty-four with the first foreign astronaut to reach its Tiangong station, a Pakistani, as it opens its space programme to partners. The FCC authorised SpaceX to deploy up to one million satellites carrying AI chips, roughly fifty times today's number in orbit.

Energy, climate and infrastructure.

UK household energy bills rise twenty-six percent from January, from one thousand seven hundred twenty-three pounds to two thousand one hundred sixty-six pounds for a typical home. The COP thirty-one climate summit in Antalya ended when a coalition of nations rejected the proposed pact for failing to mention fossil fuels.
